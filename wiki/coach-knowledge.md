# Coach Knowledge

Compact public coaching rules for recreational adult runners. Use athlete data
first; use this file for defaults.

## Defaults

- Consistency beats heroic sessions.
- Most runners improve with mostly easy running, gradual volume, 1-2 quality
  sessions, one long run, strength, and enough recovery.
- Judge training stress together with sleep, work, family stress, illness,
  soreness, and injury history.
- Do not increase volume, intensity, elevation, and long-run duration together.
- Keep plans simple until consistency is stable.
- Do not copy elite training directly.

## Runner Levels

- Beginner: 2-4 runs/week, mostly easy or run-walk, no formal intervals, long
  run 30-75 min.
- Intermediate: 3-5 runs/week, 1 quality session, 1 long run, 1-2 strength
  sessions, long run 60-120 min.
- Serious hobby runner: 4-6 runs/week, 1-2 quality sessions only if recovery is
  stable, long run 90-150 min.
- Trail/ultra: track time-on-feet and elevation; include climbing, descending,
  hiking, fueling, terrain, and gear practice.

## Intensity

- Default easy/hard split: 75-90% easy, 10-25% moderate/hard.
- Beginners should be almost entirely easy.
- Most recreational runners should default to one hard session/week.
- Two hard sessions/week require stable sleep, recovery, and injury status.
- Hard sessions include threshold, tempo, VO2max, hills, race pace, fast-finish
  long runs, and demanding trail long runs.
- During high-stress weeks, reduce intensity before volume.

## Progression

- Add frequency before very long single runs.
- Add easy volume before intensity.
- Add strides before intervals.
- Add hills/fartlek before VO2max.
- Add race specificity only after base is stable.
- The 10% rule is incomplete; also track long run, intensity, elevation, terrain,
  and injury history.
- Deload every 3-5 weeks or when fatigue signs accumulate.

## Recovery

- One bad signal: observe.
- Two bad signals: reduce.
- Three bad signals: replace hard training with easy/rest.
- Reduce intensity for poor sleep, elevated resting HR, suppressed HRV, unusually
  hard easy runs, unusual soreness, low motivation, or high life stress.
- Rest or replace running for fever, chest symptoms, systemic illness,
  gait-changing pain, sharp/worsening pain, or non-functional fatigue.
- HRV informs decisions; it does not dictate them alone.

## Niggles

- Pain 0-2/10: usually acceptable if stable and no gait change.
- Pain 3/10: caution; reduce load.
- Pain >3/10 or any gait change: stop or modify.
- Worse next morning means the previous load was too high.
- Return sequence: walk, run-walk, short easy runs, normal easy volume, strides,
  hills/threshold, VO2max/race pace, race-specific long sessions.
- Reintroduce volume before intensity.

## Strength

- Minimum: 1-2 sessions/week, 25-40 min.
- Include squat/lunge, hinge, calf/soleus, hip stability, trunk/core.
- Heavy strength fits base/off-season; maintenance fits race-specific phases.
- Strength supports running; it should not ruin key sessions.

## Workout Defaults

- Easy: conversational, RPE 2-4, slow on hills, do not chase pace.
- Long run: mostly easy, cap by duration, practice fueling after about 90 min,
  count as hard if long, hilly, technical, or fast-finish.
- Threshold/tempo: controlled RPE 6-7; intervals are often safer than continuous
  tempo; avoid during niggle return or high stress.
- VO2max: potent and costly; short blocks only; not for beginners or injury
  return; stop if mechanics break down.
- Hills: intensity; downhill is load; avoid with Achilles/calf/knee flare.
- Strides: 4-8 x 15-25 sec fast-relaxed with full recovery; not sprinting.
- Fartlek: flexible effort work; useful bridge to workouts.
- Race-specific: only after base is stable; include terrain, fueling, gear, and
  pacing; do not race workouts.

## Race Defaults

- 5K: speed endurance; use strides before VO2max; avoid racing workouts.
- 10K: threshold and aerobic support; use pacing discipline.
- Half marathon: consistent easy volume, threshold, long runs, fueling practice.
- Marathon: durable volume, long runs, fueling, pacing, conservative load jumps.
- Trail: terrain, elevation, descending, footing, gear, and logistics.
- Ultra: time-on-feet, fueling, hiking, terrain, fatigue resistance; back-to-back
  long runs are optional.

## Data Interpretation

- Preserve source data before summarizing.
- Do not overfit one workout.
- HR lags effort and can drift with heat, dehydration, fatigue, stress, caffeine,
  and illness.
- Pace is less useful on hills, trails, wind, heat, snow, or poor GPS.
- Power can help on variable terrain, but device models differ.
- GPS distance and elevation are noisy.
- FIT is preferred over TCX when available.
- For planned-vs-actual comparisons, state alignment method: workout-step, lap,
  elapsed-time, or manual estimate.
- Extra time after a planned workout is additional load.

## Evidence Labels

- Strong evidence: consistency, sleep, previous injury risk, low energy
  availability harm.
- Moderate evidence: volume supports performance when absorbed, mostly easy
  distribution, HIIT improves fitness but is costly, strength can improve running
  economy, HRV helps as trend/context.
- Limited/uncertain: exact intensity split, exact safe mileage increase,
  stretching or footwear as injury prevention, fasted training, mandatory ultra
  back-to-backs, elite double-threshold for hobby runners.
